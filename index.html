Co-op Work Term Report

Ranking and unranking PCR3, the Granny de Bruijn sequence

A term spent under the supervision of Professor Joseph Sawada, working through an open problem in combinatorial generation to its complete proof.

01 — Introduction

What this report covers

This term I worked with Professor Joseph Sawada on an open problem concerning PCR3, the Granny de Bruijn sequence: finding efficient algorithms to rank and unrank it. By the end of the term I had a technique, the lemmas needed to support it, an implementation, and a complete proof that the algorithm is correct.

02 — The employer

Working in an academic research group

Rather than an industry placement, this term was spent as a research assistant in Professor Sawada's group at the University of Guelph. The group works on combinatorial generation — the design and analysis of algorithms that list, rank, and unrank complex combinatorial objects such as de Bruijn sequences, Gray codes, and related structures. It's a small, close-supervision environment: most of the term was spent working directly with my supervisor on a single open problem, rather than moving between separate assigned tasks.

03 — Job description

The problem, and what I did with it

The task was to find ranking and unranking algorithms for PCR3, the Granny de Bruijn sequence. This was an open problem going into the term. I was responsible for the full arc of the work: developing the technique, identifying and proving the lemmas it depended on, implementing it in code, and verifying the implementation against the theory.

By the end of the term, the algorithm was complete and fully proven, and I was writing it up as a paper. Although I did a great deal of the math work to prove these theorems, I owe real credit to my supervisor Joe Sawada, whose support and ideas were essential in resolving a nontrivial proof I couldn't work out on my own as well as offering ideas for simplification.

04 — Goals

Learning goals for the term

1

Solving an open problem

Critical & creative thinking — problem solving

Coming into the term, I wanted to make real progress on an unsolved problem rather than just apply known methods to known ones. My plan was to work through the relevant literature and see which techniques I already knew could be adapted to it, treating success as reaching a solution with code that actually verified it, not just a promising idea. That's how it played out: I found a solution and backed it up with working verification, which told me the approach held up under scrutiny and not just on paper.

2

Writing a cohesive paper

Communicating — written communication

I set the bar high for myself here: write a paper cohesive enough that my supervisor would consider it ready to submit to a journal. The plan was straightforward, I would write, get feedback, revise, repeat — but the actual writing turned out to be a much bigger gap between my technical understanding and my ability to explain it clearly than I'd expected. It's the one goal where the result fell short of what I set out to do, and honestly the more useful lesson of the two: proving something and communicating it well are different skills.

3

Reading research papers effectively

Communicating — reading comprehension

Reading combinatorics papers at the start of the term was slow going, so the goal was simply to get faster and more comfortable at it, mainly by reading everything connected to the problem I was working on and measuring my own progress against where I started. The improvement was real if modest; a recent case where I got through part of a paper on a topic I had almost no background in is the clearest sign it worked, even if I wouldn't call myself fluent yet.

05 — Conclusion

Where things landed

Going in, ranking and unranking PCR3 was an open problem. By the end of the term, I'd built and fully proven an algorithm that does both, backed by an implementation that checks out against the proof. The paper is still being finished, but the mathematical result itself is complete. If I had to point to one thing from this term, it's that: taking a problem from open to proven, start to finish.
